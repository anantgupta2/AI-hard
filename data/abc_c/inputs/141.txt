ABSTRACT: We prove that the existential theory of the reals lies in the counting hierarchy. More generally, we show that the truth of existential–universal sentences over the reals can be decided in a fixed level of the counting hierarchy, even when the integer polynomials are given by arithmetic circuits.
[theorem] \label{thm:main} There is an absolute integer $j$ such that \[ \EATR\in\Clevel j. \] The level $j$ is independent of the input length, the numbers of variables, the degrees of the circuit polynomials, and their coefficient magnitudes.

[proposition] [Sign from residues]\label{prop:crt-sign} Let $X(w)\in\Z$ and suppose that nonnegative short integers $m(w)$ and $\lambda(w)$ are computable in polynomial time, with $|X(w)|\le2^{m(w)}$. Define \begin{equation}\label{eq:crt-bounds} Z=\max(16,m,\lambda),\qquad Y=2Z^4. \end{equation} If $X(w)\bmod p$, represented in $\{0,\ldots,p-1\}$, is uniformly computable in $\Flevel{a}$ for primes $\lambda<p\le Y$, then $\sgn X(w)$ is in $\Flevel{\max(a,5)+3}$.

- Our algebraic tools belong to the critical-point and deformation methods of real algebraic geometry~\cite{BasuPollackRoy2006}. Multiplication determinants are evaluated through the classical relation between residues and traces~\cite{SchejaStorch1975,CattaniDickensteinSturmfels1996}. We prove the needed identities and computational rules directly, including their behavior under parameter specialization. These computational rules apply to implicitly represented families that need not have short coefficient lists or arithmetic circuits.
- The polynomials used below can have exponentially large degrees and coefficients with exponentially many bits. Their full expansions are therefore unsuitable as algorithmic data. We retain instead three pieces of information: a degree bound, a coefficient-norm bound, and a procedure for evaluation modulo a prime. This section develops the counting operations that preserve this description and recover the sign of an integer from it. The modular-product and approximate Chinese-remainder methods follow the arithmetic approach of Allender et al.~\cite[Section~4]{AllenderBurgisserKjeldgaardPedersenMiltersen2009}; we prove the forms, including the oracle bounds, that we need.
