SUBJECT: Theoretical computer science

=== MANUSCRIPT: An Almost-Linear Approximation Scheme for Edit Distance (September-24-2026) [preprints/An-Almost-Linear-Approximation-Scheme-for-Edit-Distance-September-24-2026/build/paper.tex]
ABSTRACT: We give a uniform randomized approximation scheme for unit-cost edit distance. For every fixed rational $`\varepsilon\in(0,1)`$, it estimates the distance between arbitrary explicitly stored strings of total length N within a factor $`1+\varepsilon`$ with probability at least 2/3, in worst-case expected time $`N^{1+o(1)}`$ on a logarithmic-word RAM. The algorithm supports polynomially bounded integer alphabets and returns zero deterministically on equal strings.
INTRODUCTION (start): \label{sec:intro} The unit-cost edit distance $\ED(x,y)$ is the minimum number of single-symbol insertions, deletions and substitutions that transform a string $x$ into a string $y$. All three operations have cost one. The strings are stored explicitly; write $N=|x|+|y|$. Their symbols are integer labels of polynomial magnitude in $N+2$, stored in words of $O(\log(N+2))$ bits. There is no bound on the alphabet size independent of $N$. An approximation scheme receives a rational $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$ and estimates the distance multiplicatively. Its guarantee must therefore include returning zero when the two strings are equal. We prove the following result. [statement] The program is uniform in the accuracy input. The asymptotic assertion is pointwise in each fixed rational accuracy: its constants and the threshold at which the bound applies may depend on $\varepsilon$. We do not assert polynomial dependence on $1/\varepsilon$ or a joint bound when the accuracy varies with $N$. The output is a distance estimate; no edit-script reconstruction is needed. The algorithm uses exact computation below an accuracy-dependent input-length threshold. Section~\ref{sec:geometry} gives the finite input convention and fallback rules; their thresholds are very large, so the result is an asymptotic approximation scheme. \subsection{Context and approach} Edit distance originates in the study of errors in transmitted strings, including Levenshtein's binary codes for insertions, deletions and bit flips~\cite{Levenshtein1966}. The dynamic program of Wagner and Fischer~\cite{WagnerFischer1974} computes the minimum cost of insertions, deletions and substitutions in quadratic time. Masek and Paterson~\cite{MasekPaterson1980} applied block tabulation to obtain a logarithmic speedup for fixed alphabets and suitable fixed costs. Distance-sensitive exact algorithms offer another route when few edits are needed: Ukkonen~\cite{Ukkonen1985} developed bounded-distance methods, and Landau and Vishkin~\cite{LandauVishkin1988,LandauVishkin1989} combined furthest-reaching states with fast matching queries. Myers's related difference algorithm~\cite{Myers1986} treats insertion--deletion scripts. For unrestricted exact computation, Backurs and Indyk~\cite{BackursIndyk2018} showed that a strongly subquadratic deterministic algorithm would refute the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis. That lower bound does not rule out fixed-factor approximation. Approximation algorithms progressively improved the accuracy available with substantially less work. In the following historical comparisons, write $n$ for a common upper bound on the string lengths. Batu and coauthors~\cite{BatuEtAl2003} gave sublinear tests for fixed alphabets that distinguish sufficiently small distance from linear distance. For general pairs, Bar-Yossef, Jayram, Krauthgamer and Kumar~\cite{BarYossefEtAl2004} obtained an $n^{3/7}$ approximation in quasi-linear time; Batu, Erg\"un and Sahinalp~\cite{BatuErgunSahinalp2006} improved the factor to $n^{1/3+o(1)}$. For binary strings, Ostrovsky and Rabani~\cite{OstrovskyRabani2007} constructed a low-distortion embedding into $\ell_1$, and Andoni and Onak~\cite{AndoniOnak2012} obtained an efficiently computable $2^{\widetilde O(\sqrt{\log n})}$ approximation in $n^{1+o(1)}$ time. Andoni, Krauthgamer and Onak~\cite{AndoniKrauthgamerOnak2010} then achieved a $(\log n)^{O(1/\xi)}$ factor in $n^{1+\xi}$ time for each fixed $\xi>0$, using a hierarchical alignment representation and nonuniformly allocated precisions. The next advances reached constant factors. Chakraborty, Das, Goldenberg, Kouck\'y and Saks~\cite{ChakrabortyEtAl2020} gave a constant-factor approximation in $\widetilde O(n^{12/7})$ time. Brakensiek and Rubinstein~\cite{BrakensiekRubinstein2020} and Kouck\'y and Saks~\cite{KouckySaks2020} independently obtained near-linear exponents for sufficiently far input pairs, with a material additive error on general pairs. [theorem] \label{thm:main} There is one uniform randomized algorithm that, given $x,y$ and a rational $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$, returns a nonnegative integer $\widehat D$ such that \[ \Pr\bigl[\ED(x,y)\le \widehat D\le(1+\varepsilon)\ED(x,y)\bigr] \ge \frac23. \] For every fixed rational $\varepsilon$, its worst-case expected running time on strings of total length $N$ is $N^{1+o(1)}$ as $N\to\infty$ on the logarithmic-word RAM. The expectation is over internal randomness only, and the bound includes input processing, preprocessing, numerical work, random sampling and all data-structure operations. Equal strings return zero deterministically.
