FAMILY 242: Single-fold Diophantine representations and undecidability under an at-most-one-solution promise
SUBJECT: Mathematical logic
OPENAI SUMMARY: Every recursively enumerable set of tuples of natural numbers has a Diophantine representation with exactly one auxiliary solution for each member and none for nonmembers. This proves the single-fold conjecture and hence the finite-fold conjecture. Diophantine solvability over the nonnegative integers remains undecidable even with an at-most-one-solution promise.

ABSTRACT: Every recursively enumerable set of natural-number tuples has a polynomial Diophantine representation with exactly one complete auxiliary tuple for each member. This proves the single-fold conjecture and, consequently, the finite-fold conjecture.
INTRODUCTION (start): Throughout, $\NN=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$. A set $S\subseteq\NN^n$ is \emph{recursively enumerable} if a deterministic computation accepts exactly its members; it may run forever on other inputs. A \emph{Diophantine representation} of $S$ is a polynomial \[ P\in\ZZ[A_1,\ldots,A_n,W_1,\ldots,W_m] \] such that $a\in S$ if and only if $P(a,w)=0$ for some $w\in\NN^m$. The representation is \emph{single-fold} if this complete tuple $w$ is unique whenever it exists. It is \emph{finite-fold} if the set of such tuples is finite for every input. These definitions count all auxiliary variables, including those used to express subsidiary arithmetic operations. [statement] The Davis--Putnam--Robinson theorem established exponential Diophantine representations of recursively enumerable relations: the defining expressions may use exponentiation as well as addition and multiplication \cite[Section~1]{dpr1961}. Robinson had shown how suitable growth conditions yield an existential definition of exponentiation \cite[Section~3]{robinson1952}. Matiyasevich's 1970 construction of an exponentially growing Diophantine relation completed this route to polynomial representations \cite[Sections~1 and~3]{mati1970}. The resulting representation theorem implies the negative solution of Hilbert's tenth problem, since a decision procedure for polynomial solvability would decide membership in every recursively enumerable set. It does not bound the number of witnesses. Matiyasevich obtained a different strengthening in 1974: every recursively enumerable relation has an exponential representation with a unique complete auxiliary tuple \cite[Section~2, Main Theorem]{mati1974}. The same paper already formulates the remaining problem of polynomial representations with this uniqueness and reduces it to single-fold binary exponentiation \cite[Section~1]{mati1974}. This is the single-fold conjecture; allowing finitely many witnesses gives the finite-fold conjecture \cite{mati2010}. Theorem~\ref{thm:main} proves the single-fold assertion over $\NN$. The witness domain matters: Miller and Shlapentokh obtain single-fold definitions of computably enumerable sets of rational integers using witnesses in characteristic-zero polynomial rings, and finite-fold definitions of computably enumerable relations in polynomial rings over rings of integers of totally real number fields \cite{millershlapentokh2022}. Our arithmetic construction develops the classical strategy of recovering powers from Pell solutions and controlling their indices through growth bounds. Cantone, Cuzziol, and Omodeo give a recent account, including a paired-Pell representation and the problem of replacing its exponential cutoff \cite[Section~6, Lemma~6.1 and Theorem~5; Section~7]{cco2024}. We supply a cutoff using rational points on a rank-one elliptic curve. Poonen uses rank-one curves and denominator growth to obtain Diophantine definitions between rings of integers \cite[Theorem~1 and Section~2.3, Lemma~8]{poonen2002}. Here the N\'eron--Tate height compares the full logarithmic x-coordinate height with a quadratic function of an integer index, with uniformly bounded additive error \cite[Chapter~VIII, Proposition~9.1 and Theorem~9.3]{silverman}. The bounded additive error makes the exact index selection below possible. The computational construction uses the bit-masking method of Jones and Matiyasevich \cite{jm1984,jm1991}. Large-base encodings turn a finite register-machine history into finitely many integers, and Lucas's binomial congruence tests the permitted binary digits. Determining the base from the input and computation time already appears in Chaitin's account \cite[Sections~2.3 and~2.6]{chaitin1987}. We use a clock and a dyadic interval to select one base for the entire run. [theorem] \label{thm:main} For every integer $n\ge1$ and every recursively enumerable set $S\subseteq\NN^n$, there are an integer $m\ge1$ and a polynomial $P\in\ZZ[A_1,\ldots,A_n,W_1,\ldots,W_m]$ such that, for every $a\in\NN^n$, \[ \#\{w\in\NN^m:P(a,w)=0\}= \begin{cases}1,&a\in S,\\0,&a\notin S.\end{cases} \]
[corollary] [Undecidability with an at-most-one-solution promise] \label{cor:unique-h10} There exist integers $m,D\ge1$ for which no algorithm has the following property: on every input polynomial $F\in\ZZ[W_1,\ldots,W_m]$ of total degree at most $D$ satisfying the promise \[ \#\{w\in\NN^m:F(w)=0\}\le1, \] the algorithm halts and correctly decides whether this set is nonempty.
